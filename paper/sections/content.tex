\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================

Signal Temporal Logic (STL) is a standard formalism for specifying temporal
properties of real-valued signals~\cite{maler2004monitoring,fainekos2009robustness}.
Its quantitative \emph{robust} semantics measures how strongly a behavior
satisfies or violates a specification, and the resulting margin underpins runtime
monitoring~\cite{bartocci2018survey}, falsification~\cite{annpureddy2011staliro},
and learning-enabled design~\cite{raman2014mpc}.

The robust semantics is defined over an idealized continuous signal, yet tools
usually evaluate it on finite, discretely sampled traces, including finite
prefixes accumulated during runtime monitoring.
This requires evaluator-level choices that are not determined by the STL formula and samples alone, such as interpolation and end-of-trace behavior.
We call these choices \emph{implicit semantics}; \cref{tab:divergence} organizes the main sources of divergence.

Because these choices are often embedded inside each tool, two STL tools
can report different robustness values or Boolean satisfaction verdicts for
the same formula and trace.
Such discrepancies have appeared in practice: validation in the ARCH-COMP 2021
falsification-tool competition had to account for different robustness
encodings of discrete gear predicates, alongside other validation
mismatches~\cite{ernst2021archcomp}. Addressing such cases requires identifying
the evaluator choice responsible for a discrepancy, locating where its effect
first appears in the formula, and tracing how that local difference propagates
to the reported result (\cref{sec:discrepancies}).

We propose \tool,%
\footnote{Artifact repository: \url{https://github.com/midoriao/tidystl}.}
a diagnostic toolkit for localizing such discrepancies.
Rather than comparing final outputs alone, \tool evaluates the same formula and
trace under named tool behaviors, aligns their intermediate robustness values
along a shared formula structure, and reports where they first diverge
(\cref{fig:arch}).
The current implementation can diagnose a recorded monitor prefix as a finite
trace; incremental evaluation as new samples arrive is future work.

This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We organize the implicit semantic choices in STL robustness
    evaluation and show that they can affect robustness values and Boolean satisfaction verdicts (\cref{sec:background,tab:divergence-profile}).
  \item We propose \tool, with a common interface and representation for STL evaluator behavior, allowing both existing tools and new evaluator variants to share a single formula-and-trace frontend (\cref{sec:design,fig:arch}).
  \item We turn cross-tool discrepancies into diagnosable evaluation differences by localizing
  where two evaluator behaviors first diverge.
  We demonstrate this with compatibility backends for Breach~\cite{donze2010breach}, TaLiRo~\cite{annpureddy2011staliro}, and RTAMT~\cite{nickovic2020rtamt}
    (\cref{sec:discrepancies,tab:e2-localization}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Related Work.}
Most STL tools follow the classical robustness of
Donz\'e and Maler~\cite{donze2010robust} or Fainekos and
Pappas~\cite{fainekos2009robustness}. Classical implementations include Breach
for verification and parameter synthesis~\cite{donze2010breach}, S-TaLiRo for
falsification~\cite{annpureddy2011staliro}, and RTAMT for online
monitoring~\cite{nickovic2020rtamt}; \tool compares evaluator-level choices across
these uses. Beyond classical robustness, some
tools further implement extended semantics or logics:
STLCG++~\cite{kapoor2025stlcgpp} for smooth robustness,
mstlo~\cite{thomsen2026mstlo} for the RoSI~\cite{deshmukh2017online}-style finite-prefix uncertainty,
CauMon~\cite{CauMon} for causation, and MoonLight~\cite{bartocci2023moonlight}
for spatio-temporal properties. Supporting a broader range of these semantics
is future work.

We take the quantitative STL semantics of Donz\'e and
Maler~\cite{donze2010robust} and the closely related framework of Fainekos and
Pappas~\cite{fainekos2009robustness} as our theoretical reference. This setting
underlies most existing STL monitoring and falsification
tools~\cite{ernst2021archcomp}. We focus on its evaluator-level variations and
recall only the definitions needed to delimit them; the cited works give the
full semantics.

\begin{definition}[Signal]
\label{def:signal}
A \emph{signal} over a set of variables $\mathbf{Var}$ is a function
$\sigma \colon \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{Var}}$.
For $\mathtt{x}\in\mathbf{Var}$, we write $\sigma_{\mathtt{x}}(t)$ for the
$\mathtt{x}$-component of $\sigma(t)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[STL syntax]
\label{def:stl}
STL formulas are generated by the following grammar:
{%
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{4pt}%
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{4pt}%
\[%
  \varphi ::= {}
    \top \mid f(\vec{x}) \geq 0
    \mid \neg\varphi \mid \varphi_1 \vee \varphi_2
    \mid \varphi_1 \wedge \varphi_2
  \mid \varphi_1 \Until_I \varphi_2
    \mid \varphi_1 \Release_I \varphi_2
    \mid \Eventually_I\varphi \mid \Always_I\varphi.
\]%
}%
Here $f$ is a function symbol, for example
$f(\mathtt{x}, \mathtt{y}) = 4\mathtt{x} - \mathtt{y} - 3$, and $I$ is a non-singular interval in $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, for example $[2,5]$, $(1,3]$, or $[0,\infty)$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Informally, the robust semantics $\rho(\varphi, \sigma, t)$ replaces the Boolean
verdict with a signed real-value,
e.g., $\rho(\mathtt{x} - 3 \geq 0, \sigma, t) = \sigma_\mathtt{x}(t) - 3$.
The Boolean connectives and temporal operators then aggregate the robustness values using $\min$, $\max$, $\inf$, and $\sup$; for example,
$\rho(\Always_{[0,2]}\,(\mathtt{x} - 3 \geq 0), \sigma, t)
  = \inf_{t' \in [t,\,t+2]} (\sigma_\mathtt{x}(t') - 3)$.

We call a design choice in a concrete evaluator's implementation an \emph{implicit semantic choice} if
the choice can change the resulting robustness evaluation even for the same formula and trace.
For example, an evaluator often receives only a finite \emph{timed state sequence}
$(t_0, \vec{v}_0), \ldots, (t_n, \vec{v}_n)$ of timestamped samples, whereas
the reference semantics is defined over an idealized signal.
How this finite representation is interpolated into a signal over $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is
an implicit semantic choice.
\Cref{tab:divergence} organizes the choices we consider.

\begin{example}[Verdict flip from one implicit choice]
\label{ex:verdict-flip}
Consider the \emph{signal beyond horizon} choice of \cref{tab:divergence}.
Let $\varphi = \Eventually_{[2,4]}(\mathtt{x} \geq 0)$ be evaluated at $t = 0$ on a trace ending at $T = 1$, whose final sample assigns $3$ to $\mathtt{x}$. The temporal window $[2,4]$ lies entirely beyond the trace. A clamping rule treats the last sample as persisting and reports $+3$ (satisfied), while a pessimistic rule treats the window as unavailable and reports $-\infty$ (violated). Thus, the same formula and trace can receive an opposite Boolean verdict depending on this choice.
\end{example}

Neither rule is intrinsically wrong: a finite trace underdetermines the
idealized signal. Rather than declaring one value correct, a discrepancy can
therefore be diagnosed by localizing the implicit choice that produced it.

\section{Design and Implementation}
\label{sec:design}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/architecture.pdf}
\caption{Architecture of \tool: a stable core shared by every backend and a
swappable backend layer (lowering and node interpretation) that isolates the
implicit semantic choices.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

This section turns the implicit semantic choices of \cref{sec:background} into
inspectable implementation structure. \tool exposes robustness evaluation
through a uniform interface, while placing choices that vary across evaluators
behind a backend boundary.

% ------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evaluation Interface}

\tool has two explicit inputs: formula and signal.
A \emph{formula} is a typed syntax tree, parsed from the textual STL frontend.
A \emph{signal} contains one or more named, batched value sequences over a shared time grid.

An optional third input, the \emph{backend}, encapsulates the implicit semantic
choices and the execution strategy. The call
\texttt{robustness(formula, signal, backend)} returns an array of robustness
values over time:

\begin{pycode}
from tidystl import Signal, parse, robustness
import numpy as np

phi = parse("G[0,5](F[0,2](velocity >= 10))")

times = np.linspace(0, 10, 200)
velocity_batch = np.array([np.sin(times), np.cos(times)])  # two traces
sig = Signal.from_dict(
    times=times,
    values={"velocity": velocity_batch},
)
rho = robustness(phi, sig)                    # default backend
rho = robustness(phi, sig, backend="breach")  # Breach-compatible
\end{pycode}

\noindent
Keeping this interface small serves two purposes. For users, the same formula
and signal can be evaluated under different documented backends without
changing the surrounding application. For backend authors, new evaluation
behavior can be implemented behind a stable interface rather than requiring a new parser,
signal representation, or calling convention.

% ------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Architecture}

Internally, \tool separates a stable core from backend-specific evaluation logic, as shown in \cref{fig:arch}.
The stable core is shared by every backend, and it does not decide how $(\varphi, \sigma)$ maps to a robustness value.
Those decisions are delegated to the backend layer.

This separation makes intermediate evaluation \emph{observable}. Because two
backends build their outputs on the same shared formula (syntax-tree)
representation, those outputs can be aligned at corresponding formula nodes.
A post-order walk can therefore identify minimal divergent nodes, whose
primitive operations and intermediate signals remain available for inspection.
\Cref{sec:discrepancies} demonstrates this diagnostic workflow.

Each backend lowers the formula AST into a backend-specific computation DAG (directed acyclic graph),
and then interprets its typed primitive operations as a concrete signal-processing
pipeline. This keeps signal-processing factors separate from the syntactic
and recursive handling of STL formulas.
\Cref{fig:inspectable-dag} illustrates
such a lowering on an example formula with nested temporal operators.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\scalebox{0.85}{\input{figures/inspectable-dag}}
\caption{
Lowering of $\varphi :\equiv \Always_{[0,5]}\bigl((\mathtt{x} \ge 0) \wedge
\Eventually_{[0,2]}(\mathtt{y} \ge 0)\bigr)$ from AST (left) to computation DAG (right).
Each DAG node is a primitive operation: the conjunction reduces pointwise while the

}

\paragraph{Extension points.}

A backend defines how the formula is lowered into a computation DAG and how
the resulting DAG nodes are interpreted. Extensions may introduce new node
types during lowering or override how existing nodes are interpreted.
The artifact repository includes the user manual (\texttt{docs/usage.md}) and
reference implementations. In \cref{sec:implement-variations} we show that
emulating an existing tool requires only compact backend-specific changes.

\paragraph{Architectural byproducts.}
Beyond this paper's scope of cross-tool discrepancy diagnosis,
the swappable architecture (\cref{fig:arch}) also enables new evaluation strategies to be implemented
through the same interface: the codebase ships differentiable
(smooth robustness~\cite{kapoor2025stlcgpp}) and SIMD-optimized backends as
instances of this extensibility.

The artifact repository includes the implementation used in our
empirical study, together with scripts and documentation (\texttt{extra/experiments/}).}
First, diagnostic cases profile finite-trace choices in the reference tools.
Second, we assess backend modularity, compatibility with reference-tool outputs,

\subsection{Backend Modularity, Compatibility, and Performance}
\label{sec:implement-variations}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================

Each compatibility backend consists of fewer than 200 lines of variant-specific
code: 85 for Breach, 167 for TaLiRo, and 193 for RTAMT. Blank and comment lines
are excluded. For Breach, the common DAG representation already suffices, so only
node-interpretation overrides are needed. TaLiRo and RTAMT instead require
custom lowering to represent tool-specific operations with dedicated DAG nodes
(e.g., normalizing TaLiRo predicates by the coefficient-vector norm and
converting RTAMT temporal intervals to discrete sample offsets), as well as
custom node interpretation. All changes remain within the backend boundary,
leaving the stable core and frontend unchanged. These counts describe
implementation size, not reverse-engineering effort.

\paragraph{Compatibility validation.}
For each compatibility backend, we generate outputs from the
corresponding reference tool on a suite covering its operators. Reproduction
tests then evaluate the same formulas and traces with the backend and compare
robustness at every reported timestep for Breach and RTAMT, and at $t=0$ for
TaLiRo, whose interface returns only a whole-trace scalar. Agreement on this
suite reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk of mismatches elsewhere.

\paragraph{Performance.}\label{sec:performance}
We benchmark
$\Always_{[0,5]}((\mathtt{x}>0)\wedge\Eventually_{[0,2]}(\mathtt{y}>0))$ on synthetic signals
sampled at equally spaced points over $[0,10]$.
\Cref{tab:scaling-main} reports single-trace and batched results for
$T=101$ and $1001$.
This benchmark indicates that per-node inspectability remains practical: the
inspectable backends are competitive with RTAMT and STLCG++ on single traces,
while vectorization remains effective across batches.

Using the validated compatibility backends, the localizer compares aligned
per-node traces on the shared syntax tree. A single post-order walk reports a
\emph{minimal divergent node}---a lowest node whose backend
outputs disagree while all of its children still agree---and its first
divergent timestep.
Backend pairs whose lowerings differ structurally are compared at the shared
syntax-tree level; predicate-internal arithmetic is treated as a leaf.
Here and below, tool names abbreviate their validated compatibility backends.
Localization identifies the first divergence between these backend implementations;
fixture agreement does not imply that the reference tools use the same internal
factorization.

\paragraph{Why not diagnostic cases alone?}
For profiling evaluator choices, diagnostic cases provide a lightweight
black-box approach. As \cref{tab:divergence-profile} demonstrates, a separating probe can classify an
evaluator on a known implicit-choice axis. Yet profiles characterize only the
tested inputs and assumed choices. When two profiles differ on several axes, a
target formula may activate only a subset---or an interaction---of them; the
profile alone cannot determine which explains an observed mismatch or where it
first arises. A compatibility backend requires reverse engineering and
validation, but exposes aligned per-node traces in the original context.
Diagnostic cases therefore complement rather than replace localization. Probes
cheaply identify known choices, whereas emulation localizes concrete
discrepancies. Using probes to refine a backend is future work.
\ifextended{\Cref{sec:app-identifiability} quantifies this limitation within a
finite hypothesis class.}{}

\begin{example}[Off-grid window localization]
\label{ex:window-sampling}
Consider the following CPS-style requirement:
\[
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
  \Always_{[0,3]}\Bigl((\mathtt{mode} \ge 0) \wedge \Eventually_{[0,1]}\bigl((\mathtt{enable}
  \ge 0) \wedge \Always_{[1,2]}(\mathtt{level} \ge 0)\bigr)\Bigr) \wedge
  (\mathtt{safety\_margin} \ge 0).$}
\]
The formula combines a nested temporal response with the independent constraint
$\mathtt{safety\_margin}\ge0$.
The outer $\Always_{[0,3]}$ requires the system to remain in the required mode
and reach an enabled point within one time unit. From that point,
$\mathtt{level}$ must remain nonnegative at offsets $[1,2]$.
On the gap from $t=0$ to $t=3$, the innermost $\Always_{[1,2]}$ has no sampled
point to reduce over. As \cref{fig:e2-window-sampling} shows, this is the first
node where Breach and TaLiRo differ, three temporal levels below the root; both
Boolean verdicts remain satisfied, so final verdict comparison would miss it.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Until verdict flip]
\label{ex:until-boundary}
For the bounded-until formula
$(\mathtt{x} \ge 0)\Until_{[1,2]}(\mathtt{y} \ge 0)$ in
\texttt{until\_boundary}, Breach and TaLiRo report $-5.0$, while RTAMT reports
$+2.0$. The localizer pins the discrepancy to the single $\Until_{[1,2]}$
node---RTAMT's discrete-time until composition.
\end{example}

ARCH-COMP illustrates a concrete use. Profiling would expose the reported
$\pm 1$ versus $\pm\infty$ encodings for the discrete gear predicate in AT5*.
If a mismatch remained in a larger formula, a corresponding backend could test
node-wise whether that difference propagates to the
result~\cite{ernst2021archcomp}.

The off-grid example shows why robustness values must be compared when verdicts
agree; the until example shows that an implicit choice can flip a verdict. Both
localize the root-level mismatch to the operations involved. Choices remain
use-dependent: interpolation may match the physical signal model, while
conservative horizon handling may suit incomplete traces. Reproducing a
participant's verdict requires its profile; comparing or ranking robustness
across participants requires a common profile.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================
\tool exposes implicit choices at the STL evaluator boundary. Separating formula
syntax, signal data, backend lowering, and DAG interpretation pinpoints the
operations where evaluations first diverge. Future work will broaden the
supported semantics, including online evaluation, and expand the validation
suite.
