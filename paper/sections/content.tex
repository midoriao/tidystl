\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================

Signal Temporal Logic (STL) is a standard formalism for specifying temporal
properties of real-valued signals~\cite{maler2004monitoring,fainekos2009robustness}.
Its quantitative \emph{robustness} semantics measures how strongly a behavior
satisfies or violates a specification, and the resulting margin underpins runtime
monitoring~\cite{bartocci2018survey}, falsification~\cite{annpureddy2011staliro},
and learning-enabled design~\cite{raman2014mpc}.

The robustness semantics is defined over an idealized continuous signal, yet a tool
usually evaluates it on a finite trace---and a formula and trace do not determine a
robustness value on their own. Each tool fixes the rest, silently; we call this
\emph{implicit semantics} and organize the choices in \cref{tab:divergence}.
These choices are not cosmetic: they can flip a tool's verdict and propagate
downstream.
Prior cross-tool studies surfaced such disagreements by post-hoc independent
checking~\cite{ernst2021archcomp}; our work closes this gap, making the
responsible choice explicit and switchable before evaluation.

We present \tool,%
\footnote{Artifact repository: \url{https://github.com/midoriao/tidystl}.}
a diagnostic toolkit that does exactly this.
It places the implicit choices behind a
backend boundary, so the same formula and signal can be evaluated under named,
swappable backends while the parser, formula representation, and public interface
stay fixed (\cref{fig:arch}). Each backend exposes its choices as inspectable
intermediate computations; compatibility backends emulate existing tools for
cross-tool diagnosis, and the same separation makes the evaluator layer extensible
(\cref{tab:variation}).

This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We organize the implicit semantic choices in STL robustness
    evaluation and show that they materially affect acceptance, verdicts, and
    rankings (\cref{sec:background,sec:discrepancies}).
  \item We present an architecture that represents these choices as explicit,
    swappable backends and makes their computations inspectable
    (\cref{sec:design}).
  \item Building on it, we emulate Breach~\cite{donze2010breach}, RTAMT~\cite{nickovic2020rtamt}, and TaLiRo~\cite{annpureddy2011staliro} as compatibility backends
    and \emph{mechanically localize} their discrepancies to the responsible
    implicit choice and formula node (\cref{tab:divergence-profile,sec:discrepancies}).
  \item We show that the architecture provides an extensible evaluator layer:
    representative variations require modular backend changes while the
    formula, signal, and public interface remain fixed
    (\cref{sec:implement-variations}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Related Work.}
In this paper, we focus on the comparison of \tool{} with Breach, RTAMT, and TaLiRo, which employ the quantitative STL semantics of Donz\'e and Maler~\cite{donze2010robust} or Fainekos and Pappas~\cite{fainekos2009robustness} as their underlying theory.
\cref{section:detailed_comparison} lists some other related tools.
Extending \tool{} to support other quantitative STL semantics, such as the RoSI semantics~\cite{deshmukh2017online}, is an interesting future direction.

We take the quantitative STL semantics of Donz\'e and
Maler~\cite{donze2010robust} as a representative theoretical reference.
Together with the closely related framework by Fainekos and Pappas~\cite{fainekos2009robustness},
it represents the practical setting used by existing STL monitoring and falsification tools~\cite{ernst2021archcomp};
our focus is on evaluator-level variations within this setting.
To fix the scope, we recall only the definitions needed to delimit where
implicit semantic choices arise in evaluation.
For completeness, Appendix~\ref{sec:formal}
records the standard full inductive definition and further scope distinctions.

\begin{definition}[Signal]
\label{def:signal}
A \emph{signal} over a set of variables $\mathbf{Var}$ is a function
$\sigma \colon \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{Var}}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[STL syntax]
\label{def:stl}
STL formulas $\varphi \in \mathbf{Fml}$ are given by:
\[
  \small
  \mathbf{Fml} \ni \varphi ::= {}
    \top \mid f(\vec{x}) \geq 0
    \mid \neg\varphi \mid \varphi_1 \vee \varphi_2
    \mid \varphi_1 \wedge \varphi_2
  \mid \varphi_1 \mathbin{U_I} \varphi_2
    \mid \varphi_1 \mathbin{R_I} \varphi_2
    \mid \Diamond_I\varphi \mid \Box_I\varphi .
\]
Here $f$ is a function symbol, for example $f(x, y) = 4x - y - 3$, and $I$ is a non-singular interval in $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, for example $[2,5]$, $(1,3]$, or $[0,\infty)$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Informally, the robust semantics $\rho(\varphi, \sigma, t)$ replaces the Boolean
verdict with a signed real-value,
e.g., $\rho(x - 3 \geq 0, \sigma, t) = \sigma_x(t) - 3$,
which the connectives and temporal operators aggregate by $\min$, $\max$, $\inf$, and $\sup$, e.g.,
$\rho(\Box_{[0,2]}\,(x - 3 \geq 0), \sigma, t)
  = \inf_{t' \in [t,\,t+2]} (\sigma_x(t') - 3)$.

We call an evaluator decision an \emph{implicit semantic choice} if
it instantiates robustness evaluation in a way
not determined by the formal definition together with the given formula and trace,
and can change acceptance or reported robustness.
For example, an evaluator often receives only a finite \emph{timed state sequence}
$(t_0, \vec{v}_0), \ldots, (t_n, \vec{v}_n)$ of timestamped samples, whereas
the reference semantics is defined over an idealized signal.
How this finite representation is interpolated into a signal over $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is
therefore an implicit semantic choice.
\Cref{tab:divergence} organizes the choices we consider.

\begin{example}[Verdict flip from one implicit choice]
\label{ex:verdict-flip}
Consider the \emph{signal beyond horizon} choice of \cref{tab:divergence}.
For $\varphi = \Diamond_{[2,4]}(x \geq 0)$ evaluated at $t = 1$ on a trace
ending at $T = 2$ with $x(T) = 3$, the window $[3,5]$ lies entirely past the
trace. A clamping rule treats the last sample as persisting and reports $+3$
(satisfied), while a pessimistic rule sees an empty window and reports
$-\infty$ (violated): same formula, same samples, opposite verdicts.
\end{example}

This is not a knife-edge coincidence of one trace: swept over a family of
inputs, the boundary- and terminal-step rules flip verdicts and reverse trace
rankings across a wide band of values (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-eval}).
The consequences compound when tools are compared. One tool's implicit choice
can contaminate another tool's verdict: in the ARCH-COMP falsification
competition, a tool's implicit choice of temporal and input bounds invalidated
the validation of \emph{other} tools' correct results, turning an implicit
choice into a fairness problem for the comparison
itself~\cite{ernst2021archcomp}.

\section{Design and Implementation}
\label{sec:design}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/architecture.pdf}
\caption{Architecture of \tool: a stable core shared by every backend and a
swappable backend layer (lowering and node interpretation) that isolates the
implicit choices.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

This section turns the implicit choices of \cref{sec:background} into
inspectable implementation structure. \tool exposes robustness evaluation
through a small interface, while placing choices that vary across evaluators
behind a backend boundary.

% ------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evaluation Interface}

\tool has two explicit inputs: formula and signal.
A \emph{formula} is a typed syntax tree, parsed from the textual STL front end.
A \emph{signal} contains one or more named, batched value sequences over a shared time grid.

An optional third input, the \emph{backend}, encapsulates the implicit
choices and the execution strategy. Evaluation takes the form
\texttt{robustness(formula, signal, backend)} and returns an array of
robustness values over time:

\begin{pycode}
from tidystl import Signal, parse, robustness
import numpy as np

phi = parse("G[0,5](F[0,2](velocity >= 10))")

times = np.linspace(0, 10, 200)
velocity_batch = np.array([np.sin(times), np.cos(times)])  # two traces
sig = Signal.from_dict(
    times=times,
    values={"velocity": velocity_batch},
)
rho = robustness(phi, sig)                    # default backend
rho = robustness(phi, sig, backend="breach")  # Breach-compatible
\end{pycode}

% Signals are batch-first: a signal carries $B$ traces over a shared time
% grid, and \texttt{robustness} returns an $(B, T)$ array, so falsification-style
% workloads evaluate a whole batch in one call. Switching the backend
% is the one-argument change on the last line; the formula, the signal, and the
% calling code are untouched.

\noindent
Keeping this interface small serves two purposes. For users, the same formula
and signal can be evaluated under different documented backends without
changing the surrounding application. For backend authors, new evaluation
behavior attaches below a stable interface rather than requiring a new parser,
signal representation, or calling convention.

% ------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Architecture}

Internally, \tool separates a stable core from backend-specific evaluation logic, as shown in \cref{fig:arch}.
The stable core is shared by every backend, and it does not decide how $(\varphi, \sigma)$ maps to a robustness value.
Those decisions are delegated to the backend layer.

This separation provides \emph{observability}: because two backends build
their intermediate outputs on the same shared formula (syntax-tree)
representation, those outputs can be aligned at corresponding formula nodes.
This makes it possible to identify the minimal divergent node
and inspect the signal-processing operations responsible for it.
\Cref{sec:discrepancies} demonstrates this diagnostic workflow.

Each backend lowers the formula AST into a backend-specific computation DAG,
and then interprets its typed primitive operations as a concrete signal-processing
pipeline. This keeps signal-processing factors separate from the syntactic
and recursive handling of STL formulas.
\Cref{fig:inspectable-dag} illustrates
such a lowering on an example formula with nested temporal operators.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/inspectable-dag}
\caption{
Lowering of $\varphi :\equiv \Box_{[0,5]}\bigl((x \ge 0) \wedge
\Diamond_{[0,2]}(y \ge 0)\bigr)$ from AST (left) to computation DAG (right).
Each node is a primitive operation: the conjunction reduces pointwise while the
temporal operators aggregate over their annotated window. Edges carry
intermediate robustness signals that remain inspectable in the evaluation result.
}
\label{fig:inspectable-dag}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Extension points.}

A backend consists of primitive operations, a lowering rule, and an executor.
Extensions may introduce new primitive operations during lowering or override the interpretation of existing ones.
For programming details, the artifact repository gives the user manual (\texttt{docs/usage.md}) and reference implementations. In \cref{sec:implement-variations}, we demonstrate that emulating an existing tool's behavior requires only backend changes with little effort.
A performance sanity check finds the overhead of this structure negligible
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-eval}).

\paragraph{Architectural byproducts.}
The swappable architecture (\cref{fig:arch}) also supports capabilities outside
the central scope of this paper.
For example, it can accomodate advanced semantic extensions, such as smooth
differentiable robustness~\cite{kapoor2025stlcgpp}, as well as
performance-optimized backends, all through the same interface.
The codebase also contains differentiable and SIMD-optimized backends as instances of this extensibility.

We evaluate \tool in two steps.\footnote{The artifact repository provides reproduction instructions (\texttt{extra/experiments/}) for all experiments; Appendix~\ref{sec:app-eval} records supplementary protocol and setup details.}
First, we show that realizing an implicit choice in \tool is a modular change
confined to the backend layer, and that the resulting compatibility backends
faithfully reproduce existing tools (\cref{sec:implement-variations}).
Second, building on these validated backends, we localize cross-tool discrepancies
to the responsible choice and formula node (\cref{sec:discrepancies}).

We fix a single set of reference tools throughout: Breach 1.11.4, RTAMT 0.3.5
(discrete default and dense, $\mathrm{RTAMT}_d$), and TaLiRo (SVN revision 146).
The implicit choices of \cref{sec:background}
already produce discrepancies on those tools: \cref{tab:divergence-profile} profiles
three finite-trace axes and every one splits the tools into at least two camps,
with no tool wrong.

The discrepancies of \cref{tab:divergence-profile} have a mechanical cause that
\tool can name. Using the validated backends of \cref{sec:implement-variations},
its localizer compares a diverging pair node by node: every backend lowers the
formula into one shared syntax tree with inspectable per-node outputs, so a
single post-order walk reports the \emph{minimal divergent node}---the lowest
node whose backend outputs disagree while all of its children still
agree---together with the primitive operation each backend runs there
(\cref{tab:e2-localization}).

%\paragraph{Divergence without a verdict flip.}
Consider the \texttt{window\_sampling} case of \cref{tab:e2-localization}, the
conjunction
\[
  \Box_{[0,3]}\bigl((\mathit{mode} \ge 0) \wedge \Diamond_{[0,1]}((\mathit{enable}
  \ge 0) \wedge \Box_{[1,2]}(\mathit{signal} \ge 0))\bigr) \wedge
  (\mathit{safety\_margin} \ge 0).
\]
It can be read as a small CPS-style monitoring requirement with a mode signal,
an enabling signal, a monitored continuous signal, and a safety margin: during
the window $[0,3]$, the system must remain in the required mode; within the
initial window $[0,1]$, it must become enabled; once enabled, the monitored
signal must stay above its threshold throughout the following $[1,2]$ window;
and the run must maintain a nonnegative safety margin. We evaluate
it on a non-uniform trace whose grid jumps from $t=0$ straight to $t=3$ (samples
at $t = 0, 3, 4, \dots, 9$), with $\mathit{mode} = \mathit{enable} = 10$ and
$\mathit{safety\_margin} = 9$ held constant and
$\mathit{signal} = [3, 0, 5, \dots, 5]$.
Breach reports root robustness $+2.0$ and TaLiRo $+5.0$: both satisfy the spec,
yet the values differ, so no flipped verdict betrays the divergence.

The localizer pins the gap to the buried $\Box_{[1,2]}$, three temporal levels
down. Evaluated at the first step, this operator reduces $\mathit{signal}$ over
the window $[1,2]$, which lies entirely inside the $0 \to 3$ grid gap and so
contains no sample. Breach interpolates $\mathit{signal}$ at the window start and
returns $+2.0$ (because linear interpolation between the samples at $t=0$ and
$t=3$ gives $\mathit{signal}(1)=2$); TaLiRo finds the window empty and returns
the always-identity
($+\infty$), so that branch stops constraining the result. The
two local readings propagate unchanged up the three nesting levels: at the root
Breach's interpolated $+2.0$ stays binding, while TaLiRo's value rises to the
next active constraint, $+5.0$.
The cause lives entirely in how an off-grid temporal window is reduced; a more
readable walkthrough of this example is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-eval}.

%\paragraph{Localizing a discrepancy to its cause.}
The verdict can also flip outright. On the bounded-until case
$(x \ge 0)\mathbin{U_{[1,2]}}(y \ge 0)$, Breach and RTAMT disagree on the root
sign (robustness $-5.0$ vs.\ $+2.0$; across three tools RTAMT is the lone
outlier), and the localizer pins every pairwise disagreement to the single
$U_{[1,2]}$ node, i.e.\ RTAMT's discrete-time until composition. The analogous contamination in
the ARCH-COMP competition, by contrast, was traced down only by manual
investigation~\cite{ernst2021archcomp}. In both cases the cause lives in the
temporal semantics, which a reader cannot recover from the formula alone.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% ================================================================
\tool makes the implicit choices of STL robustness evaluation explicit at the evaluator boundary.  By separating formula syntax, signal data, backend-specific lowering, and DAG interpretation, it exposes choices that are otherwise buried in tool internals and mechanically localizes the resulting cross-tool discrepancies to minimal divergent formula nodes and responsible operations.

The implementation supports the view of robustness evaluation as an extensible semantic layer rather than an implementation detail of a particular monitor, falsifier, or learning pipeline.  Future work will extend this layer to broader semantics, including online evaluation, and expand its validation suite.
